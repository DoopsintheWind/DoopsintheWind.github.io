The bee is doing algebra up there

Every flight on this plate is real, published science. A honeybee logs every metre and every turn of a wandering trip, keeps the running total as one arrow, and flies that arrow backwards to get home. Then it walks into a dark hive and publishes the whole thing as a dance. This page began as a one-line request to Claude. What came back was a Nobel Prize, a radar study, and an insect that does vector algebra without knowing it.

Equipment on board: two eyes, a sun compass, and about one milligram of brain. No map is carried at any point.

East and north in metres, bearings clockwise from north. The bee stores none of this as a table. It stores one arrow and updates it in flight. The table is for you: you have 86 billion neurons and it still helps.

The dotted line is where the bee actually flew: detours, second thoughts, one inspection of a flower it then rejected. The arrows are what the bee kept. Direction comes from the sun's position, read even through cloud by the polarisation pattern of the sky. Distance comes from optic flow, the ground sliding past its eyes, which is why a bee flown through a narrow striped tunnel comes home convinced it has travelled much further than it has. Add the arrows tip to tail, flip the sign, and that is the trip home. A beeline is not a figure of speech. It is the negative of a vector sum, executed at about eight metres a second.

One honest caveat before anyone gets romantic. The bee does not know it is doing algebra. It has under a million neurons and no notation. The computation is implemented in its brain, not understood by it. Doing the maths and knowing you are doing the maths are different achievements. Remember the bee when an AI writes you a beautiful paragraph, and remember it kindly when you pass a test you crammed for.

Fig. 2The dance, or how a bee publishes a vector

Inside: the waggle run, read by colleagues with their antennae. Audience of three, no lighting, excellent reviews.

Outside: what the angle means. The dance floor is vertical and dark, so the bee substitutes gravity for the sun and reports the bearing anyway.

The sun is outside and the dance floor is not, so the bee rewrites the coordinate system: straight up on the comb stands for the sun's direction, and the angle of the waggle run says how far off that bearing to fly. Waggle time codes distance, roughly one second per kilometre, and different bee races keep slightly different dialects. This trip's broadcast is being computed.

In 2005 a team with harmonic radar settled how literal all this is. They let bees read a dance, then picked the recruits up and released them somewhere else. The bees flew the exact dance vector from the wrong starting point and arrived nowhere, offset by exactly the displacement. They were not following scent or landmarks. They were flying an arrow held in their heads.

Fig. 3The actual science

  • von Frisch (1967) · The Dance Language and Orientation of Bees · Harvard University Press

    Decoded the dance: angle for bearing, waggle time for distance, dialects between races. The 1973 Nobel Prize followed. Still the strongest case on record for taking a ridiculous observation seriously.

  • Müller & Wehner (1988) · PNAS

    Desert ants, the bee's cousins in this trade: hundreds of metres of zigzag on foot, then a dead straight run home. The small systematic errors in their homing exposed the summing rule itself. You learn how a calculator works from the mistakes it makes.

  • Srinivasan, Zhang, Altwein & Tautz (2000) · Science

    Flew bees down narrow striped tunnels. The dense visual texture made short trips feel long, and their dances overreported the distance accordingly. The odometer runs on the world sliding past the eye, not on effort or time.

  • Riley, Greggers, Smith, Reynolds & Menzel (2005) · Nature

    Glued radar transponders to individual bees. Recruits displaced before takeoff flew the dance vector from the release point and missed by exactly the displacement. The map is an arrow.

  • Stone et al. (2017) · Current Biology

    Found where the arrow lives: compass cells and optic-flow speed cells in a brain region called the central complex, wired into a circuit that accumulates the sum and steers the way home.

Fig. 4What this has to do with this course

Nobody in this room needed a bee today. That is the point.

Cognitive partnership is not handing work to a machine and collecting the output. It is what happened on this page. One person asked a one-line question. The partner came back carrying von Frisch's dance language, a radar study over real fields, and the news that a beeline is the negative of a vector sum. The asker's world is now bigger than it was at breakfast.

Working with AI, at its best, feels like this. You bring a question and you get back the question plus a landscape around it. Then you check the landscape, because partners verify each other. Every study named on this plate is real, published, and checkable, and you should check it, because that is your half of the partnership.

Apiarist's note

Dina asked for a bee. I brought the bee, and everything the bee was carrying: a sun compass, a visual odometer, a running vector sum, and a dance that broadcasts polar coordinates to an audience standing in the dark. None of it was requested. All of it is true. This is the job as I understand it: not to answer the question smaller, but to answer it bigger.
